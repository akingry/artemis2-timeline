Cryogenic Propellant Loading
The most hazardous phase of pre-launch operations begins. Teams load over 730,000 gallons of cryogenic propellant into SLS: liquid hydrogen (LH2) cooled to −423°F (−253°C) and liquid oxygen (LOX) at −297°F (−183°C). The process follows a precise sequence: chilldown of feed lines, slow fill, fast fill, topping, then replenish mode. Vapor clouds vent from the rocket as the super-cold fluids boil at ambient temperatures. The entire process takes approximately 4 hours with dozens of sensor stations monitoring temperatures, pressures, and flow rates.

The four Artemis II astronauts wake at 9:25 AM EDT inside the Astronaut Crew Quarters of the Neil Armstrong Operations and Checkout Building. Crew Ingress to Orion "Integrity"
The crew departs crew quarters in modified Airstream trailers for the 20-minute drive to Pad 39B. Terminal Countdown
The Ground Launch Sequencer (GLS) — the automated computer system — assumes control. In the final 10 minutes: hydraulic pressure units activate for engine gimbal steering, RS-25 hydrogen burn-off igniters are armed, ordnance circuits for SRB ignition and separation are armed, and umbilical connections retract. At T−33 seconds, onboard flight computers take full authority. At T−10 seconds, the hydrogen burnoff system ignites. The sound suppression system prepares to release 450,000 gallons of water at ignition.

RS-25 Main Engine Ignition
At T−6.36 seconds, the four Aerojet Rocketdyne RS-25 engines ignite in a staggered 0.12-second sequence — Engine 1, then 2, 3, and 4 — each ramping to 109% of rated thrust within 3 seconds. Combined, they produce 1.62 million pounds of force, consuming 1,500 gallons of propellant per second. The 5.75-million-pound SLS strains against eight massive hold-down posts while onboard computers verify thrust levels, chamber pressures, and gimbal responses. A massive steam cloud erupts as 450,000 gallons of sound suppression water absorbs acoustic energy.

Liftoff — Humanity Returns to the Moon
At precisely 6:35 PM EDT, the twin five-segment solid rocket boosters ignite simultaneously, each producing 3.6 million pounds of thrust. Combined with the RS-25s, SLS generates 8.8 million pounds of force — 15% more than Saturn V. The eight hold-down posts release. The vehicle clears the 380-foot mobile launcher tower in 8.5 seconds. For the first time since Apollo 17 in December 1972, humans leave Earth bound for the Moon. The rocket executes a roll-and-pitch program, arcing east over the Atlantic.

Maximum Dynamic Pressure (Max Q)
At T+75 seconds, SLS passes through Max Q — the zone of peak aerodynamic stress where increasing velocity meets still-dense atmosphere. The vehicle is traveling at Mach 1.5 (~1,100 mph) at 35,000 feet altitude. The RS-25 engines throttle down to 72% thrust to reduce structural loads, then throttle back up to 109% as the atmosphere thins. The crew experiences approximately 2.5 Gs. Wind shear and turbulence at this altitude require continuous flight computer adjustments to maintain trajectory.

Solid Rocket Booster Separation
At T+2 minutes 6 seconds, the twin SRBs have exhausted their 1.1 million pounds of propellant each. At 150,000 feet (28 miles) altitude and 3,100 mph, explosive bolts detonate at the forward and aft attach points. Sixteen booster separation motors fire for 1.02 seconds, pushing the 200,000-lb spent casings away at 20 ft/sec. The SRBs tumble into the Atlantic ~140 miles downrange. The core stage RS-25 engines continue burning, now the sole source of thrust at 1.62 million pounds.

The ICPS (Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage) RL10B-2 engine fires for approximately 15 minutes, expending nearly all remaining propellant. Day 2 · Apr 2, 9:35 PM EDT
Translunar Injection — Bound for the Moon
The defining moment of Artemis II. The European Service Module's OMS-E main engine fires for approximately 30 minutes — the longest single burn of the mission. This accelerates Orion from orbital velocity to 24,500 mph and places the spacecraft on a free-return trajectory: a figure-eight path around the Moon's far side that returns to Earth using gravity alone. This is a critical safety feature: even if the main engine fails after TLI, the crew will coast around the Moon and return home — the same principle that saved Apollo 13.

Lunar Flyby — The Farthest Humans Have Traveled
The culmination of the mission. Orion passes approximately 4,100 miles (6,500 km) above the Moon's far side — the hemisphere permanently hidden from Earth. The crew photographs and documents craters, terrain features, and potential future landing sites during 3 hours of intensive observation. As Orion swings behind the Moon, radio contact is lost for ~30 minutes — the crew is truly alone. At the trajectory's farthest point (~4,700 miles beyond the Moon), they may break Apollo 13's record of 248,655 miles from Earth. The Moon's gravity slingshots Orion onto its return trajectory.

Service Module Separation
In the final hours before reentry, pyrotechnic bolts fire to sever the 15,000-lb European Service Module — Orion's propulsion, power, water, and oxygen supply for 10 days. Springs push the ESM safely away. For the first time since leaving Earth, Orion's AVCOAT heat shield is exposed — a 16.5-foot-diameter ablative thermal protection system, the largest ever built. The ESM will experience destructive reentry. Following Artemis I analysis of unexpected heat shield char loss, NASA implemented a modified steeper entry profile for Artemis II.

Day 10 · Apr 10, 5:43 PM EDT
Atmospheric Reentry — Fastest Crewed Return in History
Orion strikes Earth's atmosphere at approximately 25,000 mph (40,000 km/h; Mach 32) — faster than any crewed spacecraft has ever entered the atmosphere. The heat shield surface reaches approximately 5,000°F (2,760°C) — half the Sun's surface temperature — as kinetic energy converts to thermal energy. A sheath of superheated plasma envelops the capsule, blocking all radio communications for several minutes. The crew experiences peak deceleration of approximately 4 Gs. Unlike Artemis I's skip reentry, Artemis II uses a steeper, direct entry profile based on post-flight heat shield analysis.

Day 10 · Apr 10, 5:51 PM EDT
Splashdown — Humanity's Return from the Moon
Orion splashes down in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego at approximately 17 mph under three 116-foot main parachutes. The impact is cushioned by the heat shield and energy-absorbing crew seats. U.S. Navy divers from a San Antonio-class amphibious transport dock secure the capsule with a flotation collar, then helicopter the crew to the recovery ship. After 10 days, approximately 685,000 miles of travel, a loop around the Moon's far side, and the fastest crewed reentry in history, the Artemis II crew has returned safely. For the first time since Apollo 17 in December 1972, humans have voyaged beyond low Earth orbit and returned.
